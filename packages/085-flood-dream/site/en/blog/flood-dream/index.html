The Old Manuals Called an Overflowing River Highly Auspicious

2026-08-12 · 9 min read

You probably remember how fast the water rose.

Ankle-deep, then knee-deep, and suddenly there was no way back. Or maybe everything was already submerged, and you were watching it from somewhere high up.

You wake up with something like a disaster broadcast still playing in your head. So most people assume it's an inauspicious dream and start searching.

Open an old dream manual, though, and the first line already contradicts that.

Overflowing Water Is Listed as a Good Sign

The commonly circulated Zhougong Jie Meng (the Duke of Zhou's dream manual, a family of traditional Chinese dream interpretation texts) devotes a separate chapter to water and fire together. Gathering its water-related lines produces a surprising direction.

  • When a river or the sea overflows, it is greatly auspicious and flourishing
  • Clear floodwater is a great and favorable omen
  • Walking on water is greatly auspicious
  • Being submerged in water yourself is greatly auspicious and beneficial

Overflowing water is the highest omen here. That's the exact opposite of how it feels today.

Why would that be?

We can only guess. In a farming society, rising water meant rain had come. Drought was the thing people feared most. And when a river flooded, it carried silt that enriched the soil.

What we picture today when we hear "flood" is submerged roads and collapsed houses. Water only became a danger once people started living densely along riverbanks.

The folk dream-reading catalog in the "Dreams" entry of the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (a major reference work on Korean history and culture) doesn't include a flood entry among its examples. The lines above come from a Chinese text with a different lineage from Korean folk records.

If the water itself is what stayed with you, our water dream guide and sea dream guide, not yet available in English, cover that in more depth.

But Being Submerged Is a Different Story

The same chapter also has lines with a completely different tone.

  • When flowing water wraps around your body, there will be imprisonment and lawsuits
  • Standing on water brings misfortune
  • Falling into water and being unable to get out is inauspicious

These break from the earlier lines. Overflowing water and water that engulfs you are treated as different things.

The first line especially stands out. It isn't about falling in or being submerged — it's about being wrapped. Water winding around the body and pinning it in place was singled out as its own scene.

The last line carries a condition, too. It doesn't judge falling in on its own; it turns on whether you couldn't get back out.

We cover that in more depth in our drowning dream guide. The same structure repeats elsewhere in the same text.

So the underlying logic of this chapter looks like this:

SceneReading
Water rising, seen from a distanceAuspicious
Clear floodwaterHighly auspicious
Water wrapping around your bodyInauspicious
Falling in and not getting outInauspicious

It isn't about how much water there was — it's about your distance from it. Water rising far away is good; water touching your body and pinning you down is bad.

Clear or Murky

There's one more thing the old records kept checking about water: its color.

The line about clear floodwater being a favorable omen makes that explicit. The condition wasn't the volume of water, but its clarity.

Read in a modern light, clear versus murky water is often connected to a sense of visibility. Water you can't see into feels more frightening because the situation itself is hidden.

And in practice, far more people report murky water in flood dreams than clear water — muddy water, black water, water with things floating in it.

If you remember which one it was, it's worth writing down. It's a distinction the old texts genuinely tracked, and it still carries meaning read today.

If You've Recently Lived Through a Disaster

This part deserves a direct mention.

There's an observation that after living through a major event, dreams resembling that event can become more frequent.

A study comparing dream content during COVID-19 looked at 4,467 dreams collected before the pandemic and 3,058 dreams collected during lockdown. Dreams from the lockdown period showed increases in negative emotion, existential worry, and infection-related content, along with links to stress and anxiety levels.

That data comes from a period of infectious disease, though. No Korean study directly measuring dream frequency among earthquake or flood survivors has been confirmed.

That's the limit of what can be said here. Research does suggest that threatening dreams can appear after living through a major event or being repeatedly exposed to related news.

Yi Ik (이익), a late-Joseon Silhak scholar, wrote in his essay Monggamnon (몽감론) that what we see and hear during the day becomes the substance of dreams.

So if you've recently seen a lot of flood-related news, that may well be the raw material. That's reflection, not prophecy.

And if you've genuinely lived through flood damage, and dreams like this keep recurring while your sleep falls apart, it's worth speaking with a professional. It's a common experience, and one that help is available for.

What Kind of Scene Was It

This dream branches into quite a few different scenes.

The Water Rose Gradually

This is the most-searched version. And people often remember the speed of it.

Read in a modern light, a gradual rise is different from a sudden one. The first version gave you time; the second didn't.

It Hit All at Once

It came in like a wave, or like a wall collapsing.

Standard scales that survey typical dream themes include flood or tidal wave as a formal item. That means it's a common scene to dream about.

The House Was Submerged

In traditional interpretation, house-related entries form a thick chapter of their own. Still, an entry specifically about submersion is hard to find. Beams breaking or a roof caving in sit on the inauspicious side instead.

We cover this further in our house dream guide, not yet available in English. Goryeo-era scholar Yi Gyu-bo reportedly had a recurring dream for six or seven years of seawater flowing over his pavilion and soaking his bedding.

You Watched From Somewhere High

You didn't touch the water — you watched it from a distance.

By the logic covered above, this isn't on the inauspicious side. It's contact with the body that's read unfavorably.

Someone Was Swept Away

There's no evidence that this dream means something is about to happen to that person. This isn't a predictive dream.

People who appear in dreams aren't the actual person — they're an image assembled from your memory. It's more natural to read this as a scene created by your own worry.

The Water Was Receding

Either the flood had already passed, or the water level was going down.

An entry addressing this specific scene is hard to find in traditional interpretation. But the same text does say that when the sky clears and the rain scatters, every worry disappears. The aftermath was read as recovery.

The Most Honest Answer

Lumi

"Search online and you'll find plenty of claims that flood dreams warn of disaster.
The old entries say the opposite — that an overflowing river is greatly auspicious and flourishing.
To people back then, water meant the harvest. It's not a prophecy, of course."

Traditional dream interpretation and modern research rely on different kinds of evidence.

What's confirmed. Old dream manuals called an overflowing river or sea highly auspicious, and clear floodwater a favorable sign. Water wrapping the body or trapping someone underwater, by contrast, was read as inauspicious. Flood and tidal wave are formal items on a typical-dream-theme scale. There's research showing dream content shifts after major events.

What isn't confirmed. Flood doesn't appear as an example in the Korean folk dream-reading catalog. No Korean data on dream frequency among flood or earthquake survivors has been confirmed. There's no evidence this dream predicts disaster.

The encyclopedia itself notes, after laying out its list of dream-reading methods, that such interpretations formed naturally from many people's experiences rather than from a consistent theoretical basis.

A few points should be made clearly.

A flood dream doesn't predict a real disaster or accident, whether about you or anyone else.

A blanket claim like "rising water means wealth" is also worth treating carefully. The old entries do lean that direction, but always with conditions attached.

It's better not to warn someone based on a dream. Doing so only transfers your worry to them.

There's no verified connection between flood dreams and lottery numbers, either.

Frequently Asked Questions

My house was submerged. An entry specifically about submersion is hard to find. Under the old logic, whether the water touched your body is the real dividing line.

I only watched from somewhere high up. That isn't on the inauspicious side under the old logic. Contact with the body is what's read unfavorably.

I saw someone get swept away. This isn't a predictive dream. It's more natural to read it as a scene shaped by your own worry.

I've been seeing a lot of flood news lately. That may well be the raw material. The observation that daytime experience resurfaces at night is a long-standing one.

This dream keeps repeating. Writing down the interval between occurrences helps make sense of it later. If you've genuinely lived through flood damage, speaking with a professional is also worth considering.

Part of why this dream feels so heavy is that it seems irreversible. Fire gets put out, wind passes, but water keeps rising.

The old records, though, read that rise differently — because more water meant a better harvest that year.

It sounds strange by today's standards. But it's worth remembering that more water wasn't automatically a bad thing.

Three things worth writing down today:

  • Did the water touch you — the line the old records actually tracked
  • Was the water clear or murky
  • How fast did it rise

Start with the first line. Most of it will come back to you.

A frightening dream shrinks once you write it down.

In your head, it can feel like the whole neighborhood went under. On paper, it's usually three or four lines.
