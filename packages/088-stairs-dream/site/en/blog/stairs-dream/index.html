There's No Evidence That Climbing Up Is Good and Going Down Is Bad

2026-08-12 · 9 min read

You probably kept climbing.

One flight led to another, and the end never came into view. Or maybe it went the other way — you needed to go down but your feet wouldn't move, the stairs suddenly turned steep, or you couldn't see what was below.

Search for this dream and you'll usually find the same claim: climbing means your fortunes are rising, and going down means they're falling.

That claim doesn't hold up anywhere. It isn't in the old dream interpretation texts, and it isn't in the research either. Let's start there.

The Old Catalogs Have No Stairs Entry

Let's start with tradition.

The Wikisource text of the Chinese Zhougong Jie Meng (Duke of Zhou dream manual tradition) has no standalone entry for stairs or staircases. Plenty of climbing entries exist, but every one of them concerns mountains, trees, or rooftops.

The eight interpretive branches listed in the Dreams entry of the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (a major reference work on Korean history and culture) don't include stairs either.

That makes sense. Staircases only became an everyday structure recently. For people in earlier eras, the way up was a mountain, a tree, or a rooftop.

So there's no way to look up a stairs dream's fortune in the old records.

What survives instead are entries about climbing and falling in general.

  • Climbing a high mountain brings great fortune
  • Climbing a mountain and then falling to the ground means losing your position
  • Climbing a mountain and having it collapse is a violent, unlucky sign

One pattern stands out here. Climbing itself was read favorably. The trouble comes from falling or collapsing.

No entry reads descending as unlucky. The old catalog drew its line between climbing and falling — not between climbing and descending.

The Research Doesn't Support That Conclusion Either

The same holds true on the modern side.

One study specifically addresses stairs dreams. Published by a German researcher in 2004, it analyzes waking sensations of real staircases alongside stairs that appear in dreams.

Its nature is worth stating clearly, though: this is a case-study analysis, not a statistical one. It draws on published stairs-dream accounts and the author's own interview material, and the published version doesn't report a sample size or a frequency figure.

That means this paper can't support a claim like "X percent of stairs dreams are about climbing" or "climbing and descending differ statistically."

The Typical Dreams Questionnaire (TDQ), the standard scale used to survey common dream themes, doesn't include stairs as a separate item either — including endless staircases. The most widely used dream-theme scale simply doesn't count stairs on its own.

Related items do exist — being unable to move, being trapped, losing control of a vehicle. None of them are staircase scenes, though.

In short: the claim that climbing means success and descending means failure has no basis in either tradition or research.

So Is There Anything Worth Reading in a Stairs Dream?

There is — just not in the direction most people expect.

The study above suggests a different framework: reading stairs dreams as connected to memories of physical sensation from real staircases — the feeling of your body moving up or down, the instant your foot slips.

This is a bodily-memory explanation rather than a symbolic one. It's worth noting, though, that no experiment has confirmed a causal link.

A few related findings are worth mentioning.

Some studies have examined whether physical rocking or motion surfaces in dream content. A 2024 review of sleep sensory-stimulation research found that studies on this kind of stimulus make up only about 4% of the literature. In one study, dreams that followed a rocked-bed condition showed related content 25% of the time, versus 7% in an unrocked condition.

There's a difference, but the number of studies is small and the methods vary. That's not enough to say "physical rocking causes stairs dreams."

Sleep-posture research adds another data point. A survey of 670 people found that sleeping face-down was associated with themes of suffocation, entrapment, and restricted movement. As a cross-sectional survey, it doesn't establish cause and effect.

That's as far as the evidence goes. Whether physical sensations during sleep bleed into dream content is an active area of study, but no research connects stairs dreams to any particular fortune.

What Actually Happens in Stairs Dreams

If there's anything to read in this dream, it's the type of scene — and there are quite a few types.

The End Never Comes Into View

You keep climbing and more stairs keep appearing. You give up counting flights.

Read in a modern light, this is less about stairs than about something that won't finish — the same structure as locking a door only to find it open again, or packing a bag that never empties.

You Can't Get Down

You need to go down, but your feet won't move, the drop below looks endless, or the stairs have simply vanished.

Traditional interpretation has no entry for this. It sits close to the sensation of being trapped, though, and the TDQ has a separate item for entrapment.

If the feeling of your body being unable to move was stronger than anything about the stairs themselves, see our can't-run dream guide.

The Stairs Narrow or Give Way

You're climbing and the width shrinks, a step disappears, or the surface caves in under your foot.

Traditional interpretation treats this as closer to the collapsing category than the climbing one — there's a passage stating that climbing a mountain and having it collapse is a violent, unlucky sign.

You Tumble or Miss a Step

This belongs more to the falling dream guide than to stairs specifically. In a survey of 500 Korean adults, falling dreams ranked among the most frequently reported.

Darkness Hides What's Below

You can't see your own feet, or the space below is pitch dark.

Someone Is Climbing Behind You

This overlaps with the chase dream guide. Stairs only allow escape in one direction, which is why this combination shows up often.

Why Splitting Up and Down Sounds So Convincing

This part is worth addressing directly.

There's probably a reason the up-good, down-bad reading of stairs dreams spread so widely.

It's baked into our everyday language. Grades "go up," prices "go down," a position gets "higher," a mood "sinks." The habit of reading up as good and down as bad is embedded in the way we speak.

So reading a stairs dream the same way feels natural. Natural, though, isn't the same as evidence-based.

The old catalog did favor climbing — but the image behind it was reaching a high place by climbing a mountain, not going up one flight of stairs.

And the old catalog never marked descending as unlucky. That part appears to have been added later.

What's Worth Writing Down

This dream doesn't offer much symbolic material to work with. It does tend to leave very specific, concrete scenes, though, which makes it easy to record.

A few details are worth writing down.

  • Which staircase it was — a familiar place, or somewhere you'd never seen
  • Whether you were alone
  • Where you were headed — did you have a destination in mind
  • The stairs' condition — wide, narrow, or dark

The third one is often surprisingly blank. You were simply climbing, without knowing where you were headed.

That sensation sits in the same place as our lost dream guide. As covered there, the old records tended to place their conclusion on what you were seeking, when it came to paths.

The Most Honest Answer

"Search 'stairs dream promotion' and you'll find plenty of claims.
The old texts don't have a stairs entry. Neither does the dream-theme scale.
I'd rather tell you it's not there than pretend it is."

Traditional dream interpretation and modern research draw on different kinds of evidence. For this particular topic, both sides offer only thin direct support.

What's confirmed. The old catalog has entries for climbing mountains, trees, and rooftops, generally read as favorable. Falling or collapse while climbing is read as unlucky. Whether physical sensations during sleep bleed into dream content is an active area of research.

What isn't confirmed. No stairs entry exists in either the old texts or the standard dream-theme scale. The claim that climbing means success and descending means failure is confirmed nowhere.

The encyclopedia itself, after laying out its list of interpretive methods, states plainly that these readings formed naturally out of many people's lived experience rather than from any consistent theoretical foundation.

A few points should be stated clearly.

There's no basis for reading a stairs-climbing dream as success or promotion.

There's no basis for reading a descending dream as failure or decline either. The old catalog never marked descending as unlucky.

A dream of tumbling down the stairs doesn't predict an accident.

There's no verified connection between stairs dreams and lottery numbers, either.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a stairs-climbing dream an auspicious dream (a good-omen dream)? There's no stairs entry in the old records at all. Climbing a mountain is read favorably, but that's a mountain, not a staircase.

Is going down the stairs bad? There's no basis for that. No entry in the old catalog reads descending as unlucky.

My staircase seemed to go on forever. This scene appears in neither the dream-theme scale nor the old catalog. A modern reading treats it as a scene about something that won't finish.

I couldn't get down. This sits close to the sensation of being trapped. If your body being unable to move was the stronger feeling, this belongs to a different category.

I fell down the stairs. That belongs to the falling dream category. It ranks among the most frequently reported dreams in surveys of Korean adults.

I remember exactly how many flights there were. There's no evidence that the number of flights carries meaning. Still, numbers rarely stay this clear in dreams, so it's worth writing down.

Stairs dreams tend to linger because of repetition. A scene built around the same motion, repeated, tends to stick in memory.

But there's one thing this dream rarely leaves behind: where you were actually headed.

Today's task comes down to three lines.

  • Where you were headed
  • The stairs' condition — narrow, dark, or did it have an end
  • How you felt on waking

It's fine if the first line stays blank. Blank is a record too.

A dream that leaves only a repeated motion behind can feel like there's nothing to write.

One line about the stairs' condition is enough, though. Track it for a few weeks, and you'll start to see whether it was always the same staircase.
