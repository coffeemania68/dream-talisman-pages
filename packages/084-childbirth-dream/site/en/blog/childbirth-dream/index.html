Pregnancy Comes First, the Dream Comes After

2026-08-12 · 10 min read

The scene of giving birth was probably vivid.

And the moment you wake, you search. Is this a taemong (conception dream)? Am I pregnant? Or the opposite — you're not expecting, so why did you dream this?

Let's start with the conclusion. A dream cannot tell you whether you're pregnant or what sex your baby will be. This holds up against both the old record and modern research.

But the research on this topic is substantial, and it runs in the opposite direction from what people assume. Pregnancy doesn't get announced by a dream — pregnancy changes your dreams.

Pregnancy Genuinely Changes Your Dreams

Several studies have looked at the dreams of pregnant women, and the numbers are fairly clear.

Dream recall. A study comparing 50 pregnant women, 202 postpartum women, and 21 non-pregnant women found dream-recall rates of 88.4%, 88.1%, and 90.5% respectively. The three groups were nearly identical.

So pregnancy doesn't make you remember dreams more often. What changes is the content.

How common pregnancy-related dreams are. Pooling several studies, the share of women reporting pregnancy-related dreams comes out to roughly 67–88%.

When childbirth dreams appear. A study comparing women in early versus late pregnancy found that childbirth content was significantly more common in late pregnancy. This is a statistically confirmed difference.

Read together, the sequence becomes clear. Childbirth dreams increase as childbirth approaches. The dream isn't forecasting the pregnancy — the pregnancy, as it advances, is what tilts the dreams toward that content.

But the Findings Aren't Entirely Consistent

One point needs to be stated honestly here.

A more recent study comparing 100 women in their first trimester against a non-pregnant control group found, in the opposite direction, that first-trimester women actually reported lower interest in nightmares and dreams in general.

This runs against the studies above. So there's no single sentence that captures "this is what happens to dreams during pregnancy." It depends on the trimester, and it depends on the study.

That's as far as the evidence goes: several reports show that dream content shifts over the course of pregnancy, and there's a finding that childbirth-related content increases as delivery approaches.

Nightmares in Late Pregnancy

This section is written specifically for readers who are currently pregnant.

In a study that had participants log their dreams over 14 days, 21% of late-pregnancy women reported moderate or worse nightmares occurring more than once a week. The non-pregnant control group came in at 7%.

That's a threefold difference. An increase in nightmares during late pregnancy is not unusual.

There's data on the postpartum period too. Among postpartum women, dreams about their baby included anxious content in 75% of reports and content involving the baby being in danger in 73%. For pregnant women, the figures were 59% and 42% respectively.

The postpartum period runs higher than pregnancy itself. The rate of remembering baby-related dreams was similar between the two groups — the content is what differed.

This isn't strange. Once there's an actual person who needs care, worry naturally becomes more concrete.

That said, if dreams like this keep recurring and your sleep is falling apart, the issue may no longer be about the dreams themselves but about sleep and overall condition. Sleep is prone to disruption both during pregnancy and after childbirth. If it's difficult, it's worth consulting an obstetrician or a mental-health professional. This is common, and help is available.

If You Don't Have a Child and Had This Dream

A large share of people searching this article fall into this category — and quite a few are men.

The old lists don't even carve out this case separately. Entries dealing with the act of giving birth are rare to begin with.

Read in modern terms, giving birth is often read as an image of something coming into existence that wasn't there before — a project you've started, a responsibility you've taken on, something you're in the middle of building.

To be clear, though, this is a symbolic reading, not a verified prediction.

And what matters here is how it felt at the time:

  • Were you happy
  • Were you afraid
  • Did it feel like too much to handle
  • Did it feel unremarkable

The third option shows up more often than you'd expect. If something good arrived but felt like too much to manage, the dream may be less about a baby and more about something you've recently taken on.

Childbirth Scenes Are Rare in the Old List

Now to tradition — and the picture here runs against expectation.

The entry on taemong in the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (a major reference work on Korean history and culture) defines taemong as a folk belief treating certain dreams as a sign of pregnancy, describing the custom of reading a dream for a child's conception, sex, and future.

But no record has been confirmed that treats "the dream of giving birth" as a specific, independent entry. What actually appears in the taemong lists isn't the act of giving birth — it's mostly the act of receiving or holding something: the sun, the moon, a star, a tiger, a large snake, fruit, a flower.

The old Chinese dream manuals include a few lines connected to pregnancy:

  • Carrying something up a mountain means conceiving a distinguished child
  • Holding a small stone in your hand means bearing a distinguished child

These aren't childbirth scenes either. They're scenes of carrying something upward, or holding something in your hand.

The encyclopedia's own description of taemong points at the same thing: whatever the taemong involves, it tends to include physical contact — something coming into your embrace, something you carry back, something you bite into.

In the old record, the core of a taemong wasn't childbirth — it was the act of receiving.

How to Read the Old Sex-Prediction Entries

Lumi

"If you came here hoping to learn the sex of your baby, this next part might be uncomfortable.
But it matters, because where that classification came from matters too."

The old taemong records do include a classification that assigns a sex, and the encyclopedia states its logic directly.

Large versus small, black versus white, yang versus yin, complete versus incomplete. The larger and more complete side was read as a boy; the smaller and not-yet-complete side was read as a girl.

So a full moon meant a boy and a half moon meant a girl. A ripened chestnut meant a boy and a chestnut still in its burr meant a girl.

The encyclopedia doesn't hide the background of this classification, either — it states plainly that the conditions of a patrilineal society, dependence on children for old-age support, and the old custom of valuing sons over daughters all fed into this preference for boys. Even this classification flipped from region to region: a jujube read as a boy in Gyeonggi Province was read as a girl in Gyeongsang Province.

Some later compilations include a passage claiming that holding a baby in a dream is auspicious if it's a boy and inauspicious if it's a girl. That source, though, is a later compilation rather than a primary archive, so it should be quoted with caution.

It's worth reading as a record. It isn't something to use as a prediction.

The encyclopedia adds that the custom of taemong has been rapidly disappearing as science has advanced and rational thinking has become more widespread.

If You're Hoping to Conceive

Many readers arrive at this section specifically, so it gets its own space.

There is no medical basis for the claim that a childbirth dream can tell you whether you're pregnant. The same goes for the baby's sex — research hasn't confirmed either claim.

Taemong stories, more often than not, get attached after the fact. The usual sequence is: a child is born, and only then does someone look back and say, "come to think of it, that dream..." The order just gets told in reverse.

If pregnancy is a possibility, a test and confirmation from a medical provider come before any dream interpretation. That route is faster and accurate.

One more thing, though: if you had this dream while hoping to conceive, that still means something in its own right. Whatever you're wishing for most right now tends to show up directly in the scene.

It just isn't information.

Told Honestly

Traditional dream interpretation and modern research rely on different kinds of evidence.

What's confirmed. Several studies report that dream content shifts over the course of pregnancy. Childbirth-related content was significantly more common in late pregnancy. Nightmares in late pregnancy were reported at 21%, versus 7% in a control group. Anxious content appeared in 75% of postpartum women's baby dreams. The core of the old taemong record is the act of receiving, not the act of giving birth.

What's not confirmed. There's no basis for the claim that a dream can reveal whether you're pregnant or your baby's sex. There's no basis for the claim that a childbirth dream predicts the actual outcome of a birth. No record has been confirmed treating childbirth itself as an independent entry in the old lists.

The encyclopedia notes, after listing out its dream-interpretation methods, that these readings emerged naturally from many people's experiences rather than from any consistent theoretical foundation.

A few things should be made clear.

A dream cannot tell you whether you're pregnant. A test is the answer.

A dream cannot tell you your baby's sex, either. The old classification is a record of its era, and the encyclopedia states plainly what that classification was based on.

A childbirth dream doesn't forecast an easy or difficult delivery.

No verified connection exists between childbirth dreams and lottery numbers, either.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a childbirth dream a taemong? In the old taemong record, the core scene was receiving or holding something, not giving birth. And a dream cannot tell you whether you're pregnant.

I'm pregnant and keep dreaming about giving birth. Research shows childbirth-related content increases significantly in late pregnancy. This is common.

I've had more nightmares since becoming pregnant. A study found 21% of late-pregnancy women reported nightmares more than once a week, versus 7% in a control group. If your sleep is falling apart, consulting a professional is a good idea.

Since giving birth, I keep dreaming my baby is in danger. One data set found that 73% of postpartum women's baby dreams involved the baby being in danger, higher than during pregnancy. This can be read as worry becoming more concrete once there's an actual person to care for.

I'm not pregnant, but I dreamed of giving birth. The old lists don't separate out this case either. A modern reading treats it as an image of something new coming into existence.

I'm a man and had a childbirth dream. Not unusual. There are recorded cases of men experiencing taemong as well.

Can I find out the baby's sex this way? No. The encyclopedia states plainly that the old classification came from a custom that favored sons, and it flipped depending on the region.

This dream stays with you because the scene itself is so large. Other dreams pass by, but this one ends with something coming into existence.

And if it's something you've been hoping for, it stays with you even longer.

Today's task comes down to three lines:

  • Did you give birth, receive something, or hold something — the old record was interested in the latter two
  • How did it feel at the time
  • Is there something new you've recently started or taken on

If you're hoping to conceive, scheduling a test matters more than keeping a dream journal.

Dreams that come while you're waiting for something are worth writing down.

Collect a few months of them, and they preserve where your mind was during that time. Read back later, they become a record in their own right.
