Sharing Flowers Was Said to Mean Scattering Apart

2026-08-12 · 10 min read

The color is probably what stayed with you.

Flower dreams tend to work that way. What happened matters less than what color it was. Maybe it was in full bloom, maybe it was wilted, or maybe someone handed it to you.

Search this dream and you'll mostly land on taemong (conception dream) content first. We'll get to that later.

Open the old records first, though, and there's a rather unexpected line. Here's what it says about flowers.

Sharing Meant Splitting Apart

Gathering the flower-related lines passed down through Ming-Qing-era Chinese dream manuals reveals a clear pattern.

  • Sharing flowers with another person means scattering apart
  • When flowers held in your hand fall, illness follows
  • If someone destroys a flower, disaster comes
  • If flowers in the garden wilt, great misfortune follows
  • Planting flowers is a sign of vitality; wealth will grow

Read them together, and a rule appears.

Planting is good. Sharing, wilting, and destroying are all bad.

The first line especially stands out. Splitting a bouquet with someone reads as a warm, friendly scene by today's standards, but the old catalog read the act of dividing itself as scattering.

A flower is a complete form in a single stem. So the moment it's split apart, the old texts treated it as an entirely different story.

One thing should be made clear, though. The line about a fallen flower in your hand leading to illness does exist, but there's no evidence this dream carries health information. That's simply how people of that era read the world.

But the Widely Circulated Manual Has No Flower Entry at All

This is the strange part of the topic.

The commonly circulated edition of the Zhougong Jie Meng (the Duke of Zhou's dream manual, a family of traditional Chinese dream interpretation texts) organizes its entries into 27 chapters. The table of contents runs like this:

Heaven and Earth, Mountains and Trees, Body and Hair, Clothing and Shoes, Swords and Drums, Emperors, Houses, Doors and Wells, Gold, Silver and Silk, Mirrors and Hairpins, Boats and Carriages, Roads and Bridges, Marriage and Pregnancy, Food and Drink, Graves and Coffins, Documents and Brushes, Grief and Illness, Gods and Buddhas, Fighting, Prison, Fields and Grains, Water and Fire, Time and Bathing, Dragons and Snakes, Cattle and Horses, Turtles and Fish.

There's no flower chapter.

Trees are there. Grains are there. Fruit trees fall under the fields chapter. But flowers alone have no chapter of their own.

Opening the fields-and-grains chapter suggests why. Everything in it is about eating and planting. Ripe rice means prosperity, thriving grain means wealth, planting vegetables means a long life.

In that text's world, plants were sustenance — not something you looked at for pleasure.

The flower-related lines mentioned above come from a later collection of dream manuals. That later arrival can be read as a sign of how long it took flowers to earn a place of their own on the list.

In Korean Records, Flowers Belong to Taemong

The plant entries under folk dream-reading in the "Dreams" entry of the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (a major reference work on Korean history and culture) don't include a flower entry either. What's there instead: trees, fruit trees, and mulberry trees.

Flowers show up instead in the taemong entry.

In a regional survey of taemong imagery sorted by the child's sex, flowers fall into the plant category associated with daughters, alongside young squash, green melons, cucumbers, and chestnut burrs.

The plant category associated with sons includes red peppers, walnuts, chestnuts, jujubes, and ginseng.

The encyclopedia also states the criteria used: large versus small, black versus white, yang versus yin, complete versus incomplete. The larger, riper, more finished items were read as sons; the smaller, less ripe, unfinished items were read as daughters.

A flower is the stage before it becomes fruit. That's why it landed in the incomplete category.

The encyclopedia also openly states that this classification arose from patriarchal social conditions and the old custom of valuing sons over daughters. And even that framework flipped from region to region — a jujube dream read as a son in Gyeonggi Province was read as a daughter in Gyeongsang Province.

As a historical record, it's worth reading. It isn't something to use for prediction.

A dream cannot tell you whether you're pregnant or what sex your child will be. Taemong stories are usually attached after the fact. Taemong in general is covered in our baby dream guide.

What Kind of Flower Was It

This dream branches into quite a few different scenes.

It Was in Full Bloom

A field of flowers, a tree covered in blossoms, or a single flower blooming unusually large.

In traditional interpretation, blooming is read favorably. One entry says that flowers blooming on a withered tree means one's descendants will flourish — the same entry covered in our tree dream guide.

Read in a modern light, a dream with vivid color is uncommon on its own. Writing down exactly which color it was tends to pay off later.

It Was Wilted

In traditional interpretation, this leans inauspicious. One entry states that when garden flowers wilt, great misfortune follows.

Still, this dream doesn't predict any actual event. The weight the old entries gave it reflects how people of that era read the world.

You Received a Flower

Someone handed it to you, or you received a bouquet.

An entry specifically about receiving a flower is hard to find in traditional interpretation. But across the catalog as a whole, receiving something is generally read favorably.

You Cut a Flower

In traditional interpretation, there's one unusual entry here. A later text records that cutting a pomegranate blossom is inauspicious, because the Korean and Chinese sound for "pomegranate" (ryu) echoes the sound for "staying" or "remaining" (ryu).

This is a reading based on wordplay, and that kind of logic shows up now and then in traditional interpretation. It isn't a rule to apply directly today.

A Flower in Your Hand Fell

This is a scene the old records treated with real weight. As noted above, though, it isn't health information.

You Shared It with Someone

This is the entry that gave the article its title. The old catalog read this as scattering apart.

You Planted a Flower

In traditional interpretation, this is read most favorably of all. One entry says it's a sign of vitality, and that wealth will grow from it.

That fits the broader pattern — entries about planting in a field tend to sit on the favorable side.

It Was a White Flower, or an Artificial One

Waking up uneasy because it reminded you of a funeral is common with this scene.

In traditional interpretation, there's no entry addressing artificial flowers. And as noted in our clothes dream guide, not yet available in English, the old catalog read even wearing mourning clothes as favorable. White wasn't automatically treated as inauspicious.

If the funeral imagery itself is what stayed with you, our funeral dream guide, not yet available in English, is the more relevant read.

Why Does Color Stick With Flower Dreams?

This is worth a closer look.

Vivid color memory in dreams is less common than people assume. Flower dreams, though, seem unusually prone to it.

There's no data confirming exactly why. But one thing can be said. Unlike other dream subjects, a flower's color is essentially the object itself. A tree has its shape, a person has a face — take away the color from a flower, and not much is left.

That may be why this dream is so easy to record. Even with no event to describe, a single color word is enough.

There's no flower item on the standard scales used to survey typical dream themes, and no confirmed data measuring how often this dream occurs. So we can't say how common it actually is.

The Most Honest Answer

Lumi

"Search online and you'll find claims that a flower dream is always auspicious.
The widely circulated old catalog has no flower entry at all.
Later manuals do have one, and there, cutting and sharing were treated seriously."

Traditional dream interpretation and modern research rely on different kinds of evidence.

What's confirmed. The widely circulated 27-chapter Zhougong Jie Meng has no flower chapter. Later dream manuals do include flower entries, reading planting favorably and sharing or wilting unfavorably. In the Korean taemong catalog, flowers are classified among plants associated with daughters, on a criterion of complete versus incomplete.

What isn't confirmed. No data measures how often flower dreams occur. No standard dream-theme scale includes a flower item. There's no evidence a dream can reveal pregnancy or a child's sex.

The encyclopedia itself notes, after laying out its list of dream-reading methods, that such interpretations formed naturally from many people's experiences rather than from a consistent theoretical basis.

A few points should be made clearly.

A flower dream cannot reveal pregnancy or a child's sex. The old classification is a historical record, and the encyclopedia itself states what criteria it used.

A wilted-flower dream doesn't predict something bad.

A dream of a flower falling from your hand isn't health information, either.

There's no verified connection between flower dreams and lottery numbers.

Flower dreams linger because of their color. Even without an event, color stays.

That may be part of why flowers earned a place on the list so late. A catalog built around what to eat would naturally take time to make room for something meant simply to be looked at.

Three things worth writing down today:

  • What color it was
  • What state the flower was in — blooming, wilted, or cut
  • What you did — did you look, receive, plant, or share
