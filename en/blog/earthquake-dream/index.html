Shaking and Cracking Were Read as Two Different Things

2026-08-12 · 10 min read

The sensation was probably of the ground disappearing under you.

You were standing, but not really standing — nothing to hold onto, no idea which way to go. There's one thing that sets this apart from other disaster dreams: there's nowhere to run except more ground.

Search this dream and you'll usually run into the same line: it means the foundation of your life is shaking.

Plausible-sounding. But that sentence can't be confirmed anywhere — not in the old texts, not in research.

What the old lists do have is something far more specific. And the direction is unexpected.

Moving Ground Was Read as a Shift in Position

The classical Chinese dream manual known as the Zhougong Jie Meng (Duke of Zhou's Dream Interpretation — a dream-divination text with a long circulation history in China, distinct in lineage from Korean folk tradition) has a chapter on land and terrain. Gathering the relevant lines from a commonly circulated edition preserved on Chinese Wikisource:

  • If the ground moves, an official post will be transferred — this is auspicious.
  • If the ground cracks open, illness will follow — this is greatly inauspicious.
  • If the ground beneath your floor caves in, you will worry about your mother.
  • If you level and smooth the ground, it is greatly auspicious and prosperous.
  • If the ground is uneven, high in some places and low in others, illness is present.

The first two lines run in opposite directions.

Moving is auspicious; cracking is inauspicious.

By today's sense of things, both scenes are simply an earthquake. But the old list treated them as separate entries.

Why the difference?

We can only guess. Movement passes; a crack remains. Shaking ground stops and that's the end of it — cracked ground stays unusable afterward.

And it's worth noting that the first line's conclusion is a position being transferred. A person's standing shifts along with the ground — quite literally, a change of place.

This isn't a prophecy, though. It's a record of how people of that era read the scene.

The entry on dreams in the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (a major reference work on Korean history and culture) doesn't list earthquakes among the examples in its folk-belief dream-interpretation catalog. The closest thing is a line under natural phenomena: if you climb a mountain and the mountain collapses, misfortune follows.

Collapse Has Its Own, Separate Entries

This is a different scene — not the ground itself, but something above it giving way.

Traditional interpretation has several relevant lines.

  • If you climb a mountain and it collapses, it is a terrible omen.
  • If you climb a tree and a branch suddenly snaps, you will be hurt or killed.
  • If a roof beam suddenly breaks, it is greatly inauspicious.
  • If a roof collapses, the household will suffer misfortune.

Every one of these runs inauspicious. And they share something in common: whatever was holding things up gives way.

These are the same entries covered in our separate guides to tree dreams and house dreams.

So even within an earthquake dream, the old list drew a line based on what, specifically, collapsed. Cracked ground and a building coming down were treated as separate cases.

If You've Actually Lived Through an Earthquake

This part needs to be addressed first.

There's an observation that after living through something significant, dreams resembling that experience can become more frequent.

A study surveying dream content during the COVID-19 period compared 4,467 dreams collected before the pandemic with 3,058 dreams collected during lockdown. Dreams from the lockdown period showed more negative emotion, more existential worry, and more infection-related content, along with associations to stress and anxiety levels.

To be honest about the limits here, though: this is data from an infectious-disease period.

No Korean study directly surveying dream frequency has been confirmed among people who lived through the 2016 Gyeongju earthquake or the 2017 Pohang earthquake. Geological and recovery records from those events exist, but they aren't dream data.

So it isn't possible to say "X percent of earthquake survivors have earthquake dreams." The data doesn't exist.

What can be said is only this: research suggests that threatening dreams can appear after living through a major event, or after repeated exposure to related news.

And to anyone who has actually lived through an earthquake, one more thing: if that dream keeps recurring and it's breaking down your sleep, that isn't something to interpret — it's something to get help with. It's common, and support is available.

Earthquakes Aren't on the Standard Scale

Let's check the modern side too.

There's a 55-item scale used to survey typical dream themes. Earthquake isn't one of the items.

Adjacent items exist — falling, losing control of a vehicle, being trapped. But an earthquake itself isn't among them.

So there's no way to say how common this dream actually is, either. The data doesn't exist.

And that widely repeated interpretation — that an earthquake dream means the foundation of your life is shaking — has never been verified. It isn't in the old texts as a psychological reading, and it hasn't been confirmed in modern research.

We won't present that line as fact in this article.

It's possible to read the sensation of the ground shaking as connected to something. But that's an interpretation, not a result.

What Kind of Scene Was It

This dream branches into quite a few distinct scenes.

Just Shaking

The building and the ground were both intact, but the shaking kept going.

By the traditional axis, this is the auspicious side. It matches the line about moving ground and a transferred position.

The Ground Cracked

Something split open, gaped, or something fell through the gap.

Traditional interpretation places this on the inauspicious side. That said, there's no evidence this dream predicts illness or an accident.

A Building Collapsed

Traditional interpretation falls under the collapse entries here — beams and roofs recorded inauspiciously.

You Couldn't Stay Standing

You fell, had to crawl, or held onto something to keep from going down.

Read in modern terms, this reads less like an earthquake story and more like a scene of your body not obeying you. It shares a family resemblance with dreams of trying to run and not being able to move — covered separately in our guide to dreams of being unable to run.

You Were Evacuating

You tried to leave and the door wouldn't open, or there were too many people, or you didn't know what to grab.

If you remember what you tried to take with you, it's worth writing down. People rarely grab something at random.

It Had Already Passed

Broken things were scattered around, and it was quiet.

Traditional interpretation doesn't have a clearly identifiable entry for this scene. But the same text does contain the line about leveling and smoothing ground being greatly auspicious and prosperous — the act of putting things back in order was read favorably.

We've written about reading the aftermath as recovery in our separate guide to typhoon dreams as well.

Someone Else Was in Danger

There's no evidence that this dream means something is going to happen to that person. It isn't a premonition.

The figures who appear in dreams are images assembled from your own memory. It's more naturally read as a scene built out of worry.

Something Might Have Actually Been Shaking

This is worth pointing out.

The observation that things touching the body during sleep can bleed into a dream is an old one. Yi Ik (이익), a late-Joseon-era scholar of the Silhak (Practical Learning) school, wrote in his essay "Monggamnon" ("On the Sensations Behind Dreams") that hearing the sound of drumming while asleep produces dreams about soldiers.

So any of these could have been raw material:

  • construction happening nearby
  • a subway or a large vehicle passing by
  • a washing machine or appliance running
  • an actual, faint vibration

There's also a phenomenon where the body suddenly jerks right as you're falling asleep. It's called a hypnic jerk, and a sensation of falling or a brief flash of imagery is sometimes reported alongside it. We cover that in more detail in our separate guide to elevator dreams.

None of these can be pinned down as the cause, though. Nothing here is confirmed.

If you've been seeing a lot of disaster-related news lately, that could also be raw material. That's reflection, not premonition.

Traditional dream interpretation and modern research rely on different kinds of evidence.

What's confirmed. Old dream manuals read shaking ground as auspicious and cracking ground as inauspicious. Mountains, trees, and roof beams collapsing each have their own entries, and all of them run inauspicious. There is research showing dream content shifting after major events.

What isn't confirmed. Earthquake isn't an item on the standard dream-theme scale. No Korean data surveying earthquake survivors' dream frequency exists. The reading that "an earthquake dream means your foundation is shaking" has never been verified. There's no earthquake entry in the Korean folk-belief list either.

The encyclopedia itself notes, after laying out its dream-interpretation list, that these readings emerged naturally from many people's experience rather than from any consistent theoretical foundation.

A few things are worth stating plainly.

An earthquake dream does not predict a real earthquake. This one especially deserves to be said clearly.

There's no evidence for the "foundation shaking" reading either. It's simply a widely repeated interpretation.

A scene where someone else is in danger isn't information about that person.

No verified connection exists between earthquake dreams and lottery numbers, either.

Part of why this dream feels especially unsettling is that your baseline disappears. Other disasters at least leave somewhere to run — with an earthquake, the ground itself is the problem.

And yet the old list read that shaking as a shift in position. Strange by today's sense of things, but at least shaking didn't automatically mean collapsing.

Today, there are three things worth writing down.

  • Did it shake, crack, or collapse — the old list treated these as three different entries
  • What did you do — hold on, try to leave, grab something
  • Was there any sound or vibration nearby last night

The third isn't dream material exactly, but for this dream it's worth checking.

A frightening dream that passed through you tends to shrink once you write it down.

For this one, it's enough to note what was shaking. Collect a few weeks of entries and you'll start to see whether it was the same scene each time.
